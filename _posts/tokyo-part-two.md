The first I went to was the Ikegami Oeshiki Festival.
The company that runs my share house provided a free tour of the shrine and the festival.

Just to give a scope of the festival: it lasts three days and draws in 300,000 people over the weekend.
Wow!
As you can see from the pictures, that is not surprising.
It's bookended by a parade that parades for around six hours.

{% include img.html src='/assets/images/tokyo-part-two-2026/oeshiki-night-0001.jpg' %}
{% include img.html src='/assets/images/tokyo-part-two-2026/oeshiki-night-0002.jpg' %}

The parades are just a little different here.
There's a lot of these mando (3,000 of them for this parade) that are set up to kind of spring around when you move them.

{% include img.html src='/assets/images/tokyo-part-two-2026/thats-a-mando.jpg' caption='That\'s a-mando' %}

And there are people carrying around matoi.
They take the bottom of the pole and spin it around to make the tassels move around.
These were some sort of flag used by Edo-period firemen.
The parade-folk would take turns spinning them because they were _so_ heavy.
Frankly, a lot of my photos of these kinda sucked - hard to get the motion in low light - so you get this stylized version that is still blurry.
Deal with it.


{% include img.html src='/assets/images/tokyo-part-two-2026/oeshiki-night-0003.jpg' caption='Matoi' %}

Sometimes the mando was swapped for something more decorative. This fish was a big hit, as you can tell from the many phones ahead of me.

{% include img.html src='/assets/images/tokyo-part-two-2026/oeshiki-night-0004.jpg' caption='Fish mando'%}

The Ikegami Oeshiki Festival is a celebration that's over 740 years old, taking place at the Honmon-ji temple, to commemorate the death of Nichiren, a Buddhist monk who founded the temple during the Kamakura period (1192-1333).
Finally, a period referenced other than the Edo period.
This guy believed that his version of Buddhism was the only one that could save civilization.
Classic move to gain followers from Nichiren.


## Myoho-ji temple in Suginami
The next evening I'm on my computer when I hear a ruckus outside.
People beating uchiwa daiko drums right outside my window.
Apparently the local temple by my house was _also_ founded by Nichiren, and they were celebrating Oeshiki as well.
Two festivals in two days, just my luck!

I went to this temple because there was an event called the Golden Dragon Dance, created in 1958 to commemorate the reconstruction of the main hall.
I do not mean to be flippant, but the legend about this dragon is inscrutable.

Some guys, allegedly, found a golden statue in a river and three days later a golden dragon, who was a manifestation of [Kannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guanyin), came down from the heavens - allegedly! - and made a bunch of pine trees, which - ALLEGEDLY - signifies a good harvest?
Was the dragon visit a coincidence?
I doubt it! Three days? It's just too three to be coincidence.
Nobody can actually see the statue these guys found, except maybe some special monks.
So keep dreaming, kid!.

I'm not going to try to understand the blurry history from [tertiary sources](https://www.givingtuesday.jp/post/kinryu-no-mai-the-golden-dragon-dance).
What's important here is that the dance was cool.
Eight men play the dragon parading into the into the courtyard, getting in everyone's faces.
Tailing it was a wagon with some women playing flutes and shamisen, and man playing an ōdaiko.
The dragon enacts some dance around another man holding a lotus flower.
The lotus flower was not in the legend, as far as I can tell.
That does not bother me _at all_.
