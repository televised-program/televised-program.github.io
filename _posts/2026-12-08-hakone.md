I booked my hostel to Hakone, which is Southeast from Mount Fuji, because the reviews of the host were just so good. And when I got there I could see why!

Kana checked me in early and asked what I'd be up to. The only plan I made was to see the Hakone Open Air Museum. She instantly had suggestions on other places to go, including this cute little coffee shop ran by an older hipster gentlemen. How hipster? you may ask. We spoke about coffee, photography, and architecture. Not only that, but the coffee shop was inside an old Airstream camper trailer. Doesn't get much more hipster than that!

## Mount Kintoki
I had asked Kana for an easy hike to see Fuji-san from and she was so accommodating. She pointed me to Mount Kintoki. The next morning, I left with the confidence of knowing that this would be a breezy hike. I have since learned to never be confident. The hike took about two and half hours and I was drenched in sweat by the end. Turns out Kana hikes Mount Fuji multiple times a year, so "easy" has a different meaning to her.

{% include img.html src='/assets/images/hakone-2026/morning-view.jpg' caption='The morning view from my bed' %}

The mountain had some lore around it. A young boy with incredible strength named [Kintarō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kintarō), who later renamed himself to Kintoki, came to the mountain and befriended the animals. Legends say he would split rocks and uproot trees with his strength. On the trail there was a sign by a rock that claimed Kintoki had split it. There was another sign by a different rock that basically said "This kid _tried_ to split this rock but couldn't," which like. why even make a sign about it? Anyway, the hike up was gorgeous, check it out.

## Fuji-san

I cannot overstate how amazing it was to see Fuji-san from this mountaintop. Seeing it in person I could instantly tell why it's such a huge part of Japanese iconography. Its surroundings are mostly flat, with a subtle slope up to this gigantic, singular, snow-covered point. Simply majestic.

## Down to the lake and back up the mountain

Hakone's transportation is ran by a single company, so you just buy a pass for a couple days to get full access to the public transportation in town, which is nice. What's even nicer is that the transportation not only includes buses and trains, but ropeways, gondolas, cable cars, and even pirate ships.

{% include img.html src='/assets/images/hakone-2026/lake-0001.jpg' caption='Yar' %}

I got to ride the gondola up the mountain to briefly see the volcanic sulphur vents, which provided a dramatic setting at sunset.

{% include img.html src='/assets/images/hakone-2026/sulphur-0001.jpg' %}
{% include img.html src='/assets/images/hakone-2026/gondola-0001.jpg' %}

Just one more fact that I loved about the train there: The track down the mountain had switchbacks. Since the tracks are on such a steep part of the mountain, the train can't do switchbacks by looping the track the other way. So instead the conductor would stop the train, get out, go to the opposite side of the train, then continue down the mountain. I thought that was crazy.

## Goodbye, Hakone
Hakone was, by far, my favorite part of my trip. Kana was just the nicest lady. She noticed I had bought some lightly pickled veggies from the convenience store, so she made me some homemade pickled veggies the next day. She'd introduce each guest to each other and listen to us talk as a free English lesson. Some of us guests and her even had late night dinner and beers together. When I left, she said "I learn from you how to be sociable," which was funny to me because she seemed like the more sociable one to me. So I said "You make it easy! You bring many people joy!"

"_You_ bring _me_ joy!" Made my day.

She sent me off with a hug, and I was off to Kyoto.
