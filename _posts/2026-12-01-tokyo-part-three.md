## Reiyūkai Shakaden
When I saw this Buddhist temple on some random Instagram post, I knew I had to go. The behemoth was just too cool to miss. It stands out so strikingly against any other Buddhist temple, and the entrance looks like you're about to be swallowed by a whale.

{% include img.html src='/assets/images/tokyo-part-three-2026/shakaden.jpg' caption='It\'s giving Jabu-Jabu'%}

You can see a better view of the outside [here on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reiyūkai#/media/File:Main_hall_of_Reiyukai_as_seen_from_Tokyo_Tower.jpg). It's really something.

## Zōjō-ji Temple
This temple is located right beside Tokyo Tower, so many tourists were coming through to get pictures of the temple with the tower in the background. The temple itself was pretty massive, but we were not allowed inside.

{% include img.html src='/assets/images/tokyo-part-three-2026/zozoji-outside.jpeg' %}

Just to the side of the temple there was an area dedicated to unborn children, whether it be by still birth, miscarriage, or abortion. They all had these knitted red hats on, which is sweet.

{% include img.html src='/assets/images/tokyo-part-three-2026/zozoji-0003.jpg' %}

But a little hard to take seriously when you see one like this:
{% include img.html src='/assets/images/tokyo-part-three-2026/zozoji-0004.jpg' %}

Also, importantly to me, I got to see a giant bell being rung by a monk. The thing weighs 15 tons and is almost 11 feet tall.

{% include img.html src='/assets/images/tokyo-part-three-2026/bell.jpeg' %}

Hearing its deep ring and feeling the reverberations nearly made me cry. Every once in a while it really hits me that it's so cool that I'm here and that I can experience something like this.

## Nikkō
Sure, the blog title says Tokyo, but a quick excursion to Nikkō was necessary before leaving Tokyo since it's just about two hours away. This town came highly recommended by many people and is often a weekend excursion for Tokyoites.

I rode the limited express train over, which was good to see the views.

{% include img.html src='/assets/images/tokyo-part-three-2026/nikko-train-view.jpg' %}

### Tōshōgū Shrine
Nikkō is home to Tōshōgū Srhine, well known for its garish decorations! The ashes of the first Shogun, Tokugawa Ieyasu, who brought in the beginning of the Edo period, allegedly resides here. Although his remains are allegedly at another temple. I don't know, Buddhism is weird because they don't let you _see_ anything.

It's some of the most ornate woodwork you'll see at a temple, since it was made in this very important man's very important honor. I think that temples are supposed to be a little less vain, but for the most powerful person in Japan, I guess, we make an exception.

{% include img.html src='/assets/images/tokyo-part-three-2026/rinnoji-0001.jpg' %}

Sadly, my hostel was also around two hours away, the same amount of time from Tokyo, depending on the train schedule, so the rides took up quite a bit of my time in Nikkō. This was basically why I booked the hostel, and it was nice to decompress after the rainy day.

### Kegon Falls
My second day in Nikkō was a long trip up the mountain by bus through switchbacks. My goal was to see Kegon falls, and Kegon falls I did see!

They have an elevator that takes you down to the bottom of the falls. I'm not going to lie, when I saw the falls I thought "This is not nearly as tall as Niagara," but it turns out Kegon Falls is almost twice the height of Niagara. As you can see though, not nearly as wide, and that's what _really_ counts.

## Goodbye Tokyo!
So, that wrapped up my short little trip to Nikko. After that, I had just a couple nights before leaving my share house.

I said goodbye and thank you to my local coffee shop, NIWATORI Coffee. This place was my go-to spot for editing photos and was a hub for folks young and old. The owner's father roasted all the beans, and all the beans were fantastic. I loved it.

I said goodbye to Echigoya, the British-inspired neighborhood food joint. The brothers there were so welcoming and we'd have a little chat every time I stopped by. The curry there has been my favorite curry I've had so far here in Japan.

I said goodbye to the local Summit store, where I would buy my fruit and salads. Since being on the road I've really come to miss the affordable pre-cut fruit and salads from Summit. Some of the hostels I've stayed at have not had kitchens, so a normal grocery store all of a sudden holds fond memories.

I said goodbye to my house mates, the French architecture interns. Although we only hung out a few times, their company was much appreciated. If it were not for them, I would not have learned the French term for cotton candy: barbe à papa (dad's beard). Which, sure, written out just makes sense, but you hear a French person say it? It just sounds likes "bah-de-buh-buh-buh".

## Enough with the sap!
Boring! What's next? Hakone, where I got to ride the bullet train, ride a gondola, and see Mount Fuji from atop a mountain. One of the best parts of my trip yet!
